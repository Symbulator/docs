Symbulator:
the calculator-based symbolic circuit simulator

Roberto Perez-Franco
November 1999

Summary

Symbulator is a powerful calculator-based symbolic linear circuit simulator, designed to help circuit courses' students with the mathematical tasks of the analysis, by allowing the numerical and symbolic solution in direct current, alternating current, transitory analysis and frequency domain, accepting most linear elements and with a reusable circuit description. It has been succesfully programmed in the TI-89 BASIC language and is offered as freeware on the Internet.

The need for a calculator-based symbolic circuit simulator

For engineering students, mathematics is their work tool, not a degree objective. In engineering circuit analysis, a mathematical procedure is a method to find the solution to a given circuit, and is not an end-unto-itself. When a student meets a new circuit type, he faces two challenges. The first and most important is to understand the concept behind the circuit, the logic behind its analysis, and its applications in engineering. The second is to master the mathematical procedures that will allow him to study the answer of this type of circuits. The student must focus on the first, leaving the second to a simulation software.

When circuits are solved by hand, innumerable details such as an unseen negative sign, or a misplaced decimal point, can make the difference between a right and a wrong answer. Professional engineers can use in their work place a numerical circuit simulator in a PC, such as SPICE. Most students do not have this choice in their classroom, because to academic and economic limitations. Very often, student's errors are due to a bad mathematical manipulation of the formulas or the values, caused by tired eyes or mental pressure of the major.

On January 1999, I committed myself to create a linear circuit simulator to assist students in their classroom with the mathematical procedures of the analysis, whether numerical or symbolic, in direct current, alternating current, transitory analysis or frequency domain, allowing almost every existent linear element.

Previous works on the topic

A preliminary step in my project was to improve the best calculator-based circuit simulators available at that moment. I worked on two of them.

The first one was the numerical simulator CSim 2.61 for HP48GX calculator, created by the Finnish engineer Per Stenius in 1991. After several weeks of consulting and a many weeks of programming, I presented the numerical simulator CSim 3.01. This version was easier to use than its predecessor. I programmed at the same time two variations on the theme: one for novice students, called Assistant II, and another focused on impedance circuits, called ZAC 1.4. The algorithm, although, was not suitable for symbolical applications in the HP48GX, so I soon abandoned this course of action.

The second was the numerical simulator NFAP for HP48GX, created by Don Robinson in 1994. I wrote two variations on the theme: the Symbolic Voltage Finder and the Symbolic Current Finder. I was not satisfied: this symbolic simulators seemed to work fine with alternating current, but they had too many limitations and did not fulfill the purpose of being a complete simulator to accompany the students through their majors.

At this point, I knew it was necessary to attack the problem in a different way, with a brand-new simulator.

Symbulator is born

I named my project Symbolic Circuit Simulator, and nicknamed it Symbulator. I visualized it as such a powerful tool that it did not fit in any category of existing calculator-based simulators.

Analysis method selection

For the circuit analysis method I would use to develop the new algorithm, I considered two options: the nodal analysis method (both modified and gyrator transform) and the method of equations generation.

The nodal analysis method is powerful. Its advantages are more evident for numerical simulations. Its disadvantages are a rather complex answer-retrieving procedure in the final vector and the messy manipulation of the matrices in symbolic simulations.

The equation generation method is a new and innovative one. I met it by my friend Joe Riel, an engineer from California, author of Syrup, a MapleV-based symbolical circuit simulator. It generates and solves the equations that describe the voltages and currents of the circuit. Its main advantage is the ease to describe dependent sources and a faster handle of symbolic simulations. Its main disadvantage is that it takes a complex algorithm.

Finally, I decided to use this last method because its advantages would lead me to a much user-friendly simulator, with the simplest notation for dependent sources, transformers, and biports.

Calculator selection

For the platform calculator to develop and run the program, I considered as options the Hewlett-Packard and Texas Instruments models available.

The algebraic systems of Hewlett-Packard 48GX and 49G models lacked at that time the symbolic capabilities required by my software for equations solution and expressions manipulation. The solve() command of the 49G model was particularly weak and messy.

The Texas Instrument 89 and 92+ models had an algebraic system that allowed the symbolic manipulation and automatic substitution of previously defined expressions. This last feature would become a decisive one in the coming algorithm. Those models had the most powerful solve() command in the block. [This solve() command is used to solve both linear and non-linear simultaneous equations with real values. Complex equations are solved with csolve().]

Finally, I selected the TI-89 to be the platform for the simulator because it had all the features required for such a program, with the lowest price of TI's high-performance models.

Programming

Once I selected the method and the calculator, I started working in the Symbulator, during the 1999 spring break. First, I programmed a small and simple application for direct current analysis that allowed only the very basic linear elements: resistance and independent sources. When it was done, I carefully modified the source code to program the alternating current, frequency domain and transitory analysis applications. At the same time, I increased the number of recognized linear elements, including inductances, capacitances, ideal operational amplifiers, ideal transformers, mutual inductances and six different biports (z/y/h/g/a/b). I am still perfecting the source code.

The growing number of Symbulator users and the satisfaction comments I've received from them make me feel that the software has been a real success.

Symbulator features

Circuit description

Circuit entry for Symbulator is described using a particular notation inside a matrix with five columns. Notation rules are similar to those of SPICE, but much easier when it comes to biports, transformers and dependent sources definition. These last elements are described in the way they are presented in textbooks, with no need for any special notation for nodes of control values. This makes Symbulator much easier than SPICE as far as dependent sources are involved.

Circuit simulation

To simulate a circuit, just type in the entry line of the calculator the type of analysis you wish, followed by the circuit description or the name of the variable in which this description is stored. There are four analysis options available: direct current DC, alternating current AC, transitory analysis TR and frequency domain FD. In the case of alternating current, the radial frequency is required; it can be left in terms of a symbolic variable or expression.

It is interesting to analyze how the Symbulator works internally. Here is the idea: The program reads the circuit description, writes down the equations that describe its behavior, solves them and stores all the answers in variables with their respective names. Answers to every analysis include numerical or symbolic expressions for the voltages in every node and current through every element in the circuit. In case of direct and alternating current analysis, the program also provides the real and complex powers, respectively, consumed in every element. In case of frequency domain and transitory analysis, the program calls to the Laplace transforms routines of DiffEq program, by the Danish programmer Lars Frederiksen, who is my personal friend and, in my opinion, the best TI calculator programmer ever. Answers for voltages and currents are left as symbolic functions of frequency and time, respectivelly.

Symbulator tools

In addition to the four simulation routines, Symbulator has tools to ease even more of the tasks of students. I've written tools to find the Thévenin equivalent of a circuit (whether active or passive), the z/y/h/g/a/b parameters of a biport circuit, the gains of a linear amplifier, and also to plot the Bode diagrams of gain and phase. The two first tools work with direct current, alternating current and frequency domain, allowing to find the transfer functions of a circuit in terms of s. These tools accept symbolic elements, giving therefore symbolic answers. For power courses, I wrote tools to find the losses, the regulation and the efficiency of a real iron-core transformer given its short-circuit and open-circuit test values, and its nominal load. This tools rely in the simulation applications of Symbulator to speed up procedures that are tedious by hand, and they are very easy to use.

Symbulator limitations

Symbulator has basically two limitations: non-linear circuits and huge circuits. There are alternatingatives to heal these limitations. For the first, non-linear elements can be modeled using their linear equivalents. For the second, parts of the circuit can be reduced in terms of their Thévenin or de biport equivalents, using the respective tools if desired.

Examples of problems solved with Symbulator

Here is a selection of four linear circuit problems which were solved using Symbulator, typing just one line of commands. The examples here are for direct current, alternating current, frequency domain and for transitory analysis.

Example 1: Direct current. Given the circuit of Image #1, find the voltage in node 3.

Image #1. Circuit of Example 1.

To solve this problem using Symbulator, write in the entry line of the calculator the following line of commands.

scs\dc([e1,1,0,vin,0;r1,1,2,r1,0;r2,2,3,r2,0;o1,0,2,3,0]):v3

Image #2. TI-89 screen for Example 1.

The first part of this line (before the ":") is the command to make a direct current analysis to the circuit described between the parenthesis. Notice the simple circuit description (zeroes at the end of each row are just fill-in). The second part (after the ":") is the request for voltage in node 3. Actually, after the simulation, we can ask for any voltage, current or power of the circuit: Symbulator stores them all in the memory of the calculator under mnemonic names. In the case of a circuit like this one, which contains Op-Amps, the algorithm includes the application of a limit() command to every answer before they are stored. This simulation took 34 seconds, which is more than what one without Op-Amps takes. The requested answer is showed in the screenshot: (-r2 vin)/r1. Let's see another example.

Example 2. Alternating current. A circuit consists of a resistance and a capacitance in series. When a voltage of 240 V r.m.s. @ 200 Hz is applied, the obtained current is 1.2 + 1.6 i A. Which are their values?

To solve this problem using Symbulator, enter the following line of commands in your calculator.

scs\ac([e1,1,0,240.*sqrt(2),0;r1,1,2,r,0;cx,2,0,c,0],2*Pi*200.):solve(real(ir1)=1.2 and imag(ir1)=1.6,{r,c})

Image #3. TI-89 screen for Example #2.

The first part of the line is the command to perform an alternating current analysis to the circuit described inside the parenthesis. Please notice that symbolic values were given to the resistance and the capacitance. The second part is to request the values of the symbolic elements that will produce the expected complex current. My calculator took 24.5 seconds to solve the problem and return the right answers: 102 ohms and 5.86 microfarads (you must scroll to the right to see the rest of the answer in the calculator's screen).

Example 3: Transitory analysis. Given the circuit of Image #4, find a symbolic expression for the voltage Vc(t) if Vs=9 cos(t) and Vc(0)=2V.

Image #4. Circuit of Example #3.

To solve this problem using Symbulator, write in the entry area of the calculator the following line of commands:

scs\tr([es,1,0,9*cos(t),0;r3,1,c,3,0;r6,c,0,6,0;cx,c,0,1/10,2]):vc

Image #5. TI-89 screen for Example #3.

The first part of this line is the command to perform a transitory analysis to the circuit described inside the parenthesis. Please notice two things: 1) the node c is named with a letter, instead of a name, and 2) the capacitor's initial condition was entered in the fifth space of capacitor's row in the circuit matrix. This fifth space is intended to receive the initial conditions of capacitors and inductors in transitory analysis, and in other elements it is zero. The second part of the line is to request the voltage of node c, which is the answer of the problem. My TI-89 took 53 seconds to solve this problem (to see the complete answer we need to scroll to the right). With equal nodes and elements, the transitory analysis requires more time. Symbulator first solves the circuit in frequency domain, and then asks DiffEq to apply the inverse Laplace transform to each answer, to take back to time domain. Remember that we receive as answers every voltage and current in the circuit. There are some tricks to speed up the simulation if only a couple of answers are needed. In any event, the Symbulator is much faster and reliable than hand.

Example 4: Frequency domain. Given the circuit of Image #6, plot the gain Bode diagram of v4/v5 for frequencies between 104 y 1013 rad/seg.

Image #6. Circuit of Example 4.

To solve this problem using Symbulator, write in the entry area the following command line:

scs\fd([e1,5,0,vg,0;r1,5,1,150.,0;r2,1,0,1000.,0;

cc1,1,0,1E-10,0;cc2,1,2,3E-12,0;jd1,2,0,0.05*v1,0;

r3,2,0,2000.,0;r4,2,3,100.,0;r5,3,0,1000.,0;

cc3,3,0,1E-10,0;cc4,3,4,3E-12,0;jd2,4,0,0.05*v3,0;

r6,4,0,2000.,0]):scs\plot()

 

Images #7 and #8. TI-89 screens for Example 4.

The first part of this line is the command to perform an analysis in frequency domain to the circuit inside the parenthesis. This circuit is considerably big: it was the final project asked by my professor of Electronics II course during 1999. The second part is to activate the Bode plotter of Symbulator. The circuit simulation in my TI-89 took 3 minutes. Plot the graph took my TI-89 52 seconds. Any electrical engineering student knows that this problem would take by hand around half an hour, or more.

I hope these examples are enough as a brief sample of Symbulator's capacities. It is clear that the first three examples cannot be solved directly with SPICE. At the Symbulator site http://scs.ticalc.org you can find around fifty solved examples, each one with a special feature.

Results up to the date

Up to this date, the results obtained by Symbulator are flattering.

As a student, I have used it in six different courses in my major and I am pleased at the ease of circuit description, the accuracy of the results and the speed of the simulation. With Symbulator, I have solved in a couple of minutes some transitory analysis problems that by hand used to take me a quarter of hour or more.

I've received dozens of positive comments from satisfied users around the world, which use Symbulator as an important tool in their studies. Users download the software in the Internet free of charge, and then share it with their classmates. Professionals and students from Australia, Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Spain, United States of America (California, Kansas, Kentucky, Missouri, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Texas), Finland, France, Great Britain (including Oxford University), Israel, Italy, Norway, Czech Republic, Switzerland, among others, have reported to use and enjoy the software. A student from Texas reported that, in his classroom, twelve classmates used the simulator in a circuit theory course. I'd like to quote here Chris Riegel, an electrical engineering major and president of the IEEE chapter in the University of Kentucky: "For EE211 and EE221, Symbulator actually works better than SPICE because you get the results symbolically. Once you use Symbulator you'll see what everybody has been talking about. If you're not using Symbulator, you're working too hard."

The success obtained by Symbulator should be measured in the improvements in knowledge and performance of the hundreds of students that already use it around the globe, to focus on the concepts of the circuits they study, instead of the math involved by their analysis.

Final words

One year after the early beta's first appeared in electrical engineering classrooms, Symbulator has evolved from a simple project to become students' dream simulator. Powerful, portable and free of charge, this state-of-the-art software has minted a new verb: to Symbulate. I would like to end quoting Jay Myers, an electrical engineering major at the University of Texas at Arlington, and experienced user of Symbulator:

"This program is the premiere circuit solver for calculators. Yes, there are others, but none are better. This program is similar to SPICE or PSpice in the way it is used, and it is even easier when it comes to dependant sources and biport simulations. Once a circuit is solved, it has all the information the user would want: node voltages, element currents, element power usage, voltage drops across elements via node voltage differences. It solves symbolically, unlike almost any other circuit simulator (even PC based). This program is, hands down, the best program for circuit analysis that I have seen. Big circuits can be slow to solve, and can run the calculator out of memory. Work-arounds are out there, including tools for breaking the circuit into groups of Thévenin or biport equivalents. I could not possibly praise this program well enough. It's hard to believe that Mr. Perez-Franco is letting the world use his fantastic program free of charge. Excellent job!"
